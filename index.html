Places to go

Vetted viewing spots across Europe

Open, publicly accessible ground within reach of a station or stop, in every city where the eclipse is visible — with the horizon actually measured on the sun's exact bearing. You don't have to tell anyone where you live; just pick somewhere and go.

How each spot was checked. For every candidate I computed the sun's bearing at maximum eclipse, then measured what is in the way along that bearing: terrain from the SRTM 30 m elevation model out to 45 km, and buildings from OpenStreetMap within a corridor 2.5 km deep, using tagged heights where they exist. The verdict compares the sun's altitude against the higher of the two. Clearance is quoted in sun-diameters (the sun is 0.53° wide) because a tenth of a degree means something very different in Reykjavík than in Milan.

Step three

Where to actually stand

At sunset the sun is only a few degrees up, so your horizon matters more than anything else — more than your optics, more than your city. One building, one row of poplars, one low hill in the wrong place and you see nothing at all.

Every Street View link below is pre-aimed at the exact compass bearing of the eclipsed sun for that spot. Open it and you are looking at the horizon you will have on the day. That is the check — not my say-so. I picked these for open western sightlines and public-transport access, but I have not stood in any of them.
If you are close

Cities that can reach totality on a commuter train

Madrid misses totality by about 15 km. Barcelona misses it by about 30 km. Both are inside a regional-rail ride of the umbra. A 99.9% partial and a 100% total are completely different experiences — this is the single highest-value move available to anyone in those two cities.

Everything on 12 August

The eclipse is only half of it

A solar eclipse means a new moon, and a new moon means the darkest possible sky that night — which happens to be the night the Perseids peak. That combination is what makes this date unusual, and it is the part most people will miss.

On the Perseid rate: sources genuinely disagree. The IMO's working figure for 2026 is a ZHR of 30–50; several outlets quote 100–150. ZHR is also a idealised number — it assumes the radiant overhead and a perfect sky. Under a real dark rural sky expect roughly 40–70 meteors an hour after midnight, and far fewer from inside a city. The moonless sky is the real story here, not the rate.
Read this first

The partial phases will blind you

Never point an unfiltered telescope, camera or binocular at the sun

A 90%-covered sun is still about a thousand times too bright to look at safely. The remaining sliver is the same surface brightness as the full disc. Telescope optics concentrate that — an unfiltered scope can cause permanent retinal damage faster than your blink reflex, and it is painless while it happens.

The filter goes on the front of the telescope, never on the eyepiece. Screw-in eyepiece "sun filters" that shipped with older beginner scopes crack under concentrated heat. Throw them away.

What you actually need

  • Eyes: ISO 12312-2 eclipse glasses. Check for pinholes against a bright lamp before the day.
  • Telescope / lens / binoculars: a full-aperture solar filter that covers the entire front opening — Baader AstroSolar film or a certified glass filter. Tape it so wind cannot lift it.
  • No filter to hand: pinhole projection, or project the image from a telescope's eyepiece onto white card. Never look through it.
  • Cameras: the filter protects the sensor and shutter too, not just you. Live-view does not make an unfiltered lens safe.
The only exception: during totality itself — and only inside the path, only between second and third contact — filters come off and you look with the naked eye. That is the whole point of totality. Everywhere outside the path, including at 99.9%, the filter never comes off at any moment.
Sunset is not a filter. The sun will look tame and orange at 2° altitude. It is not. Haze varies minute to minute and can drop several stops without warning — people have been hurt precisely because a low sun felt safe to stare at. Keep the filter on until the disc is fully gone below the horizon.

How accurate is this, and where does it stop?

What is genuinely computed

Contact times, magnitude, obscuration and totality duration come from the polynomial Besselian elements NASA publishes for this specific eclipse, evaluated for your coordinates with the standard method — the same maths professional eclipse software uses. Sun altitude, azimuth and sunset are computed independently.

What this page does not know

  • Your actual horizon. This is the big one. There is no terrain or building model behind these numbers. When the page says "you need a clear horizon down to 2°", it cannot tell you whether you have one. Only the Street View links, a map contour, or your own eyes can. At these sun altitudes this is the difference between seeing the eclipse and not seeing it.
  • The weather. The cloud figures are climatology — long-run averages, not a forecast. Check an actual forecast 48 hours out and be ready to drive.
  • Refraction near the horizon. Below about 5° the sun's true position differs from where it appears by more than its own diameter, and the disc flattens visibly. Standard refraction is applied, but real refraction on the day depends on the air column.
  • Altitude. Everything assumes sea level. Being 300 m up extends the eclipse slightly and lowers your true horizon — helpful, not modelled.
Before you commit to a spot, cross-check the timings against Xavier Jubier's interactive map, which is the reference tool the eclipse-chasing community actually uses, and check your skyline with this horizon checker.

So will this get you a good eclipse?

It gets you the right place, the right minute and the right bearing, and it tells you what gear will not hurt you. It cannot get you a clear sky, and it cannot see the building at the end of your street. If you are outside the path of totality, no tool changes that — only travelling does. Use this to plan, then verify your horizon on the ground a day early, in the same evening light, at the same time.
